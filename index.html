Knowledge Hub

Practical lessons for active traders

Δ Delta

What: How much the option price moves for a ₹1 move in the index. Calls range 0 to +1, puts 0 to −1.

Use: ATM options are near 0.5. Higher delta means more responsive and costlier. It also estimates the odds of expiring in the money.

Γ Gamma

What: How fast delta itself changes.

Use: Gamma is highest for ATM options near expiry, so premiums can swing sharply. This gives buyers fast gains but gives sellers big risk.

Θ Theta

What: Premium lost every day from time passing.

Use: Decay speeds up near expiry. Buyers need the move to happen quickly. Sellers collect theta but carry unlimited-type risk without hedges.

V Vega

What: Change in premium for a 1-point change in implied volatility (IV).

Use: IV tends to be high before events (budget, results, policy) and drops after. This is called IV crush, and it can hurt buyers even when direction is right.

  1. Start on a higher timeframe. Use daily and 1-hour charts first, then drop to 15-minute for timing.
  2. Mark swing highs and lows. Points where price reversed several times are your support and resistance zones. Draw zones, not thin lines.
  3. Add previous day high, low and close. Intraday traders watch these daily because many participants do.
  4. Note round numbers. Levels like 74,000 or 23,000 often act as psychological barriers.
  5. Check option data. Strikes with high open interest often act as support (puts) or resistance (calls).
  6. Wait for confirmation. Look for a rejection candle or breakout with volume at the level before you enter.
  7. Fix stop-loss and target first. Put SL beyond the level. Aim for at least 1:2 risk-to-reward. Example: risk ₹20, target ₹40.
  8. Learn the method, not the call. In our sessions we walk through level-marking on real charts as teaching examples. They are never buy or sell recommendations.

What is liquidity?

How easily you can enter and exit at a fair price. Low liquidity causes slippage: you get a worse price than you expected.

Check before every trade

Bid-ask spread: tight is good, wide is costly.

Volume: higher traded quantity means easier exits.

Open interest: shows how many contracts are active.

Which strikes?

ATM and near-ATM strikes are usually the most liquid. Far out-of-the-money options may look cheap but are often hard to exit.

Liquidity zones on charts

Areas above recent highs and below recent lows hold many stop-loss orders. Price often sweeps them quickly before reversing, so avoid placing stops exactly at obvious levels.

Patterns work best at a marked level and with confirmation. None of them is a guarantee.
